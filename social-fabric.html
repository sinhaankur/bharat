The social fabric of Ashoka's India

An empire is not its monuments — it is its people. This is the working world of Mauryan India (c. 322–185 BCE): the priest who kept the rite, the merchant who moved the goods, the guild (shreni) that set the price, the artisan who made the thing, the farmer who fed them all, the soldier who held the frontier, and the officer (adhyaksha) through whom the state reached every one of them. Each as a crafted card — role, dress, tools, what they traded or made, and the word for them in its own script.

The order — seven classes, not four

What Megasthenes saw

The Greek ambassador Megasthenes, at Chandragupta's court, described India not by the four varnas of the Brahmanical texts but by seven working classes — philosophers, farmers, herdsmen, artisans, soldiers, overseers, and the king's councillors. It was a functional map of who did what, and it has a scholarly caveat: it is his scheme, an outsider's, and the famous four-fold varna system sits alongside the far older, finer web of jati — birth-communities and occupations — that people actually lived in.

What transpired: over the centuries the loose, functional order of the Mauryan world hardened. The shreni (guild) — once a flexible corporation of a craft that acted as bank, producer and price-setter — became entangled with birth; occupation and jati fused; and mobility narrowed. Method — a society, read as a system

Role, term, hand

We read the society the way we read a design system: each role is a component with its own token (a palette drawn from its materials — the priest's kuśa-green and sacrificial fire, the smith's iron-and-ember, the farmer's soil-and-grain), its label (the Sanskrit/Prakrit term, in Brahmi where the glyphs survive), and its tools (the atoms of the trade). We never flatten real people into a diagram — we name the source, keep the claims conservative, and take the design idea, the way the Indic design systems take the discipline of stone or bronze.

The term glyphs render in self-hosted Noto Indic fonts (Brahmi & Devanagari) already bundled with the site, so the letters are never tofu. Icons are original line-drawings in the house Mauryan style (mauryan-icons.svg).
