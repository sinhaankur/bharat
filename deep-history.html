The population shifts written in DNA

Long before written history, the people of the subcontinent were remade again and again by migration and mixture. Ancient DNA now reads those turnovers directly — who arrived, who blended, who was replaced — from Homo sapiens meeting Neanderthals to the layered ancestry (AASI, Iranian-farmer, Steppe/Yamnaya) that makes up South Asians today. Held strictly to the science; the "Aryan migration" narrative is shown where it belongs — as a debated interpretation of undisputed data.

A sharp change in a region's DNA is the hard evidence of a population shift. It proves that people moved and mixed — and sometimes that one population replaced another. It does not, by itself, prove a massacre: migration, admixture and conquest are different claims, and this page keeps them apart.

And it points the other way from division: almost every South Asian is a blend of the same handful of ancient sources, and the Steppe pulse that reshaped North India also reshaped Europe. The modern borders and communal lines are far younger than the shared ancestry beneath them — read at the level of DNA, this record shows how related everyone is, not how separate. (Shared descent is a fact; it grants no group superiority, ownership, or a claim over another.)
