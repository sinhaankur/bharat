    History & heritage · c. 3300 BCE to today

    Bharat, through five thousand years

    "India, that is Bharat" opens the Constitution. The older name comes from the Bharatas of the Rigveda and the legendary king Bharata. Scroll down the river of lamps: most of this story happens long before Delhi's sultans or the British. At each era the sparks gather into a symbol of that age.

    c. 3300 to 1300 BCE · mature c. 2600 to 1900 BCE

    The brick cities of the Indus

    Along the Indus and Ghaggar-Hakra rivers rose some of the world's first planned cities: grid streets, fired-brick houses with bathing rooms and wells, and covered drains along the main roads. Mohenjo-daro's Great Bath was likely used for ritual or public bathing.

    • Five great cities: Mohenjo-daro, Harappa, Ganeriwala, Dholavira and Rakhigarhi; Mohenjo-daro and Harappa may each have held 30,000 to 60,000 people
    • An unread script: roughly 400 to 600 signs, mostly on small steatite seals gap · undeciphered
    • Trade: standardised stone weights, and Sumerian texts mention a partner called Meluhha, most likely the Indus
    • Decline: as the climate dried and rivers turned seasonal, most cities were abandoned by about 1700 BCE

    c. 1500 to 500 BCE

    The Vedic age and the sacred fire

    The Rigveda, composed around 1400 to 1000 BCE in an early form of Sanskrit, was passed down orally with remarkable precision. Worship centred on the fire altar, यज्ञ (yajna). Its Hymn of Creation ends in open doubt about how everything began:

    "he verily knows it, or perhaps he knows not."Rigveda 10.129.7, trans. R. T. H. Griffith, 1896
    • Four Vedas: Rig, Sama, Yajur and Atharva
    • Philosophy: the early Upanishads, completed in the later Vedic era, turned to the self and reality
    • Kingdoms: by the 6th century BCE, tribal units had merged into large kingdoms, the Mahajanapadas
    In the atlas: the Hymn of Creation, word by word →

    c. 6th to 5th century BCE

    An age of new ideas

    Sixteen great kingdoms spread across the Gangetic plain. In this ferment Siddhartha Gautama found enlightenment under a peepal tree at Bodh Gaya and became the Buddha, while Mahavira taught the Jain path of non-violence.

    • The Bodhi leaf: the peepal's heart-shaped leaf became a symbol of awakening
    • Magadha: centred on modern Bihar, it rose to dominate the plain
    • The Buddha's dates: scholars still disagree on the exact years gap · debated

    c. 320 to 185 BCE

    The Mauryas and Ashoka's wheel

    Chandragupta Maurya founded an empire ruled from Pataliputra, near modern Patna. His grandson Ashoka (c. 268 to 232 BCE) conquered Kalinga around 261 BCE. In Rock Edict 13 he records 150,000 people deported and 100,000 slain, then publishes his own regret:

    "This is the repentance of Beloved-of-the-Gods."Major Rock Edict 13, trans. E. Hultzsch, 1925
    • A footprint in stone: edict find-spots run from Kandahar to Karnataka, in Brahmi, Kharoshthi, Greek and Aramaic
    • Read again in 1837: James Prinsep's decipherment of Brahmi made the edicts legible after two millennia
    • National symbols: the Sarnath Lion Capital became India's emblem in 1947, and its 24-spoke wheel sits on the flag
    In the atlas: Ashoka's rule of the land →

    c. 300 BCE to 300 CE dates debated

    The Sangam age of the Tamil south

    While empires rose in the north, the far south, Tamilakam, was ruled by the Chera, Chola and Pandya dynasties. Its poets left one of the oldest bodies of literature in any living language, written on palm leaves and compiled centuries later.

    • Sangam literature: 2,381 poems by 473 poets, at least 27 of them women
    • Love and war: the poems divide into akam (the inner world, about three-fourths) and puram (the outer world)
    • Tolkappiyam: the oldest surviving Tamil grammar sets out the rules of this poetry
    In the atlas: the languages of Bharat →

    c. 320 to 550 CE

    The Guptas and the gift of zero

    Often called India's classical age, the Gupta era peaked under Chandragupta II (375 to 415). Aryabhata, aged 23 in 499 CE, wrote the Aryabhatiya: he approximated π as about 3.1416 and stated that the Earth rotates on its axis.

    • Zero: in 628 CE Brahmagupta wrote the first known rules for arithmetic with zero and negative numbers
    • Learning: Nalanda, said to be founded by Kumaragupta I, became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2016
    • Metalwork: the Iron Pillar of Delhi still resists rust thanks to its high-phosphorus iron
    • Travellers: the Chinese monk Faxian visited from 405 to 411 and described a mild penal code
    In the atlas: walk inside Ajanta →

    c. 600 to 1200 CE

    The age of stone and learning

    Across these centuries regional dynasties turned mountains into temples and monasteries into universities. At Ellora, the Kailasa temple, generally credited to the Rashtrakuta king Krishna I (c. 756 to 773), was carved downward out of a single rock, its summit 32.6 metres above the courtyard.

    • The Palas (c. 750 to 1161): patrons of Nalanda and founders of Vikramashila; the Pala-era scholar Atisha taught in Tibet and Sumatra
    • Khajuraho: the Kandariya Mahadeva temple, c. 1030, rises about 31 metres, clustered with 84 miniature spires
    • Kashmir: the Martand Sun Temple was built in the 8th century under Lalitaditya Muktapida
    • Ideas travelled: Pala art shaped that of Nepal, Burma, Sri Lanka and Java
    In the atlas: temple forms in 3D →

    848 to 1279 CE

    The Cholas, temples and the sea

    From the Kaveri delta, the Chola dynasty built a maritime power. Rajaraja I (985 to 1014) built the Brihadisvara temple at Thanjavur between 1003 and 1010, a 13-storey stone tower over the sanctum.

    • How tall: sources give the tower as about 60 to 66 metres range · sources differ
    • Naval reach: in 1025 Rajendra I raided the ports of Srivijaya; Chola forces reached Sri Lanka, the Maldives and parts of Southeast Asia
    • Bronze: Chola craftsmen excelled in bronze sculpture, such as the dancing Nataraja
    • Living heritage: Brihadisvara is one of UNESCO's Great Living Chola Temples
    In the atlas: the Dravidian form in 3D →

    c. 1000 to 1826

    Many kingdoms, one civilisation

    Bharat was never a single throne. From Odisha to Assam to the Deccan, regional kingdoms built, traded and endured, some for centuries after Delhi changed hands.

    • Konark: the 13th-century Sun Temple of Narasimhadeva I is a stone chariot with 24 wheels drawn by 7 horses
    • Vijayanagara: founded in 1336 by the brothers Harihara I and Bukka Raya I, it peaked under Krishnadevaraya (1509 to 1529); its capital Hampi is a UNESCO site
    • Ranakpur: the Jain temple begun around 1437 stands on 1,444 carved marble pillars
    • The Ahoms: founded by Sukaphaa in 1228, their kingdom in Assam lasted 598 years, until 1826

    1206 to 1707

    Conquest, sultanates and the Mughals

    The Delhi Sultanate began in 1206. In 1526 Babur, a Timurid prince from Central Asia, defeated Ibrahim Lodi at the First Battle of Panipat and founded the Mughal Empire. Under Akbar (1556 to 1605) Persian and Indian traditions blended in art, language and building, and by about 1700 the empire reached its greatest extent.

    • Taj Mahal: commissioned by Shah Jahan in 1631 for Mumtaz Mahal, built from 1632 to about 1653 by over 20,000 workers
    • New faiths and powers: Guru Nanak (1469 to 1539) founded Sikhism; Shivaji's coronation in 1674 marked the rise of the Marathas
    • Turning point: Aurangzeb's death in 1707 is taken as the end of the Mughal imperial era
    • Framing, not record: many people remember these centuries as foreign conquest and rule; many historians separate them from British colonialism because the rulers settled here and revenue was not shipped to a home country commentary
    In the atlas: the history race →

    1757 to 1947

    Colonial rule and the freedom struggle

    After Plassey in 1757 the East India Company took Bengal; after the rebellion of 1857 the last Mughal emperor was exiled and the British Crown ruled directly. The national movement grew from 1885, and Gandhi's spinning wheel, the charkha, became its symbol of self-reliance.

    • Salt March: from 12 March to 6 April 1930, Gandhi and 78 volunteers walked about 385 km from Sabarmati to Dandi to break the salt law; over 60,000 people were jailed in the campaign that followed
    • Quit India: the movement began in 1942
    • Independence: 15 August 1947, with a Partition that displaced 12 to 20 million people and killed an estimated 200,000 to 2 million range · estimates vary
    In the atlas: the record of what happened →

    1950 to today

    The Republic, and the Moon

    The Constitution, drafted by a committee chaired by B. R. Ambedkar, was adopted on 26 November 1949 and came into force on 26 January 1950. With 395 articles at enactment, it is the longest written national constitution in the world.

    • Space: ISRO was formed on 15 August 1969; India's first satellite, Aryabhata, launched in 1975
    • Mars: Mangalyaan entered Mars orbit on 24 September 2014, the first Asian spacecraft to do so, on India's first attempt
    • The Moon: Chandrayaan-3 made the first soft landing near the lunar south pole on 23 August 2023, now marked as National Space Day
