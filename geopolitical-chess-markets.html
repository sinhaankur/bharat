Three economies, one board

Geopolitical Chess argues the US dollar is the board everyone plays on. This companion asks the next question: what does each player actually earn its living doing? Canada, the United States and India — what their real economies run on, what their stock markets actually price, and why the petrodollar privilege belongs to the country whose currency oil is priced in — not to oil producers like Canada.

⚠ Numbers are sourced · the reading is a framing

Every figure below is as-of dated and sourced (IMF, World Bank, Statistics Canada, SIFMA, S&P/TSX, NSE/BSE, NASSCOM, EIA/IEA). The interpretation — "core engine", "no exorbitant privilege", "commodity currency" — is Framing editorial reading, tagged as such. It's a model to argue with, not a verdict.

"Canada has no core industry" — half true

It does have core industries — energy, mining, banking — but they're concentrated and low-productivity. The missing piece vs the US is a large tech / advanced-manufacturing core. AI was ~30% of US GDP growth last year; ~5% in Canada.

The productivity gap

Canadian capital piled into housing and mature resources (profitable, but they don't raise output per worker). Canada spends a larger share of investment on residential housing than any OECD country.

India's bet

India is trying to build the dynamic core Canada lacks: a ~$315B tech industry (79% exported) plus a deliberate manufacturing push — the opposite of a resource-rentier model.

3 · Does the petrodollar engine work for Canada?

Short answer: no. The privilege belongs to the currency oil is priced in (USD) — not to oil producers. This is the key correction to "Canada sells oil, so it must benefit like the US."

  1. Most globally traded oil is invoiced & settled in US dollars — a convention since the 1970s, not a treaty.
  2. Every country therefore needs dollars to buy oil → constant global demand for USD.
  3. Oil exporters (incl. Gulf states, many USD-pegged) earn dollars, then recycle them into US Treasuries — "petrodollar recycling".
  4. That funds US deficit spending cheaply and lets the US effectively print the world's reserve currency — the "exorbitant privilege".

Canada earns petrodollars — it doesn't issue them

Canada is a top-4 oil producer, but its crude is priced in USD at WTI/WCS benchmarks (~90% sold to the US, at a discount). It is a price-taker on the receiving side.

The CAD is a commodity currency

The loonie historically rises and falls with oil — the opposite of a privilege. The USD is the safe haven oil is priced in; the CAD weakens when oil falls.

No "print to pay" backstop

If Canada over-prints CAD it just gets inflation + a weaker currency. There is no global demand sink to absorb it, as there is for the dollar.

On OPEC & "printed money"

OPEC is ~12–13 members (OPEC+ ~23, not 51). Gulf states don't price their currency on printed money — many peg to the USD and recycle oil dollars into Treasuries. The flow benefits the US, not the producers.

The convention is eroding at the margins: the dollar's reserve share has slipped toward multi-decade lows, and some China–Gulf oil now settles in yuan (scale disputed). But this is gradual diversification toward a multipolar currency system, not an overnight collapse — and either way it is a story about the dollar, not about Canada.

Framing Bottom line. The petrodollar privilege is a US-dollar phenomenon. Canada, despite pumping more oil than almost anyone, sits on the price-taker side with a currency that weakens when oil falls — structurally the inverse of the US position. India, a large energy importer, is exposed the other way: a strong dollar and dear oil raise its import bill.

4 · How the dollar engine actually works

"Petrodollar" is just the oldest spoke of a bigger wheel. Four mechanisms, each well-established in economics, explain why the dollar sits at the centre — and why that's both a privilege and a trap.

① Exorbitant privilege

Because the world wants dollar safe assets, the US borrows below the rate anyone else pays — a permanent subsidy ("seigniorage") that funds a standing trade deficit. And it borrows in its own currency, so it can never be forced to default: the Fed can always issue dollars. Named by France's finance minister in 1965; the Dutch (1600s) and British (1800s) had it before.

② The Triffin dilemma — the trap

To supply the world with dollars, the US must run deficits. But endless deficits eventually erode confidence in the very asset everyone holds. Robert Triffin (1960) predicted this would break the gold standard — which it did in 1971. The dilemma never left; it just changed shape. This is the structural reason "dollar dominance forever" is not guaranteed.

③ Dollar recycling — the loop

The deeper privilege isn't that oil is bought in dollars — it's that surplus countries recycle those dollars back into US Treasuries. That funds US deficits cheaply. The key insight for a geopolitical investor: even if trade keeps using dollars, the privilege erodes if countries stop parking their reserves in US assets (central-bank gold buying, BRICS).

④ The Eurodollar system — the hidden size

Most "dollars" exist offshore, created by non-US banks lending dollars to each other (~$14T+), far beyond what the Fed prints. It's why the Fed had to rescue the world in 2008 & 2020 with swap lines. It also means global dollar liquidity can vanish in a crisis — the single biggest tail risk to everything priced in dollars.

Framing The investor takeaway: the dollar's dominance is real and sticky, but it rests on trust + recycling, not a law of nature. De-dollarisation is gradual, not a switch — so bet on direction and fragility (a multipolar drift, periodic dollar-liquidity squeezes), not on a date for collapse.

5 · How geopolitics reaches your portfolio

This is the chain that connects a central-bank decision to your returns. Understanding it is the point — it's why a thesis can beat a reflexive index buy.

  1. US bond yields rise → by interest-rate parity, capital chases the higher dollar return, so the dollar strengthens now.
  2. Capital flows reverse out of emerging markets (the carry trade unwinds) — money flees back to dollar safety.
  3. EM currencies fall; dollar debts get heavier ("original sin": they borrowed in USD, earn in local currency) → a self-reinforcing doom loop.
  4. Commodity currencies (CAD) track risk + yields more than oil now; importers (India) get hit twice — dearer dollars and dearer dollar-priced oil.

Why a weak currency doesn't "fix" India

Textbook says a cheaper rupee boosts exports and self-corrects. It fails here (Marshall–Lerner): oil demand is inelastic — India buys the same barrels at a higher rupee cost. A ~$10 oil rise adds ~$14–15B to the import bill; each ₹1 of depreciation adds ~$1.6B. The rupee's fall widens the deficit, not narrows it.

Why CAD stopped tracking oil

In 2026 the loonie decoupled from crude — oil sands investment shrank as a share of GDP, and bond yields + global risk appetite now drive it more than WTI. A "commodity currency" that no longer rallies on oil is itself a signal about Canada's shifting economy.

The uncomfortable ETF truth

A cap-weighted index ETF is not neutral — a TSX ETF is ~69% banks/energy/materials, so you're already making a concentrated macro bet, just an unexamined one. "Understanding the market" means choosing exposures (country · currency · sector · duration · real assets) that match your thesis.

So is an ETF the "true answer"? Framing

Often the honest answer is: the ETF isn't the problem, the default index is. You can express a geopolitical view with ETFs — currency-hedged vs unhedged, equal-weight vs cap-weight, sector/commodity/gold/short-duration bond funds — usually more cheaply and with less single-name risk than picking stocks. The edge is in the allocation, not the wrapper.

Framing · not investment advice The through-line: yields move currencies (parity) → capital flows enforce it → dollar strength tightens the whole world, hitting dollar-debt importers hardest. Your real choices are which country, which currency exposure, which sector, and how much duration/real-asset ballast — the index just picks those for you by market cap. Decide them on purpose.

6 · Why "chess" is the wrong game

The parent page calls the dollar the board and each country a piece — a useful lens, but chess quietly smuggles in assumptions that geopolitics breaks. The ways it breaks are the insight. Framing

Chess assumes…Geopolitics actually is…
Two players, fixed sidesMany players (this board has 15), shifting coalitions, no stable "us vs them".
Fixed rulesThe strongest players write and break the rules mid-game — sanctions, SWIFT cut-offs, tariffs rewrite the board.
Perfect informationHidden intent, bluffing, deception, secret deals, intelligence failures.
Pieces have fixed powersA "pawn" at a chokepoint (Taiwan, Hormuz) can decide everything; a "queen" can be paralysed.
Players are unitaryEach piece is itself a contested board — ruling party, military, backers, public, all pulling.
Zero-sum; someone winsMostly non-zero-sum — trade enriches both sides; nobody "wins", the game never ends.
You move your own piecesA country is both player and board — acted upon while acting.
Turn-basedSimultaneous and continuous — no one waits for your move.

So which game fits better? Each captures something chess can't:

Poker

Hidden cards, bluffing, betting, reading opponents. Captures incomplete information and deception — the sanctions brinkmanship and signalling that chess, with everything on the table, cannot model.

Go (Weiqi)

China's own strategic metaphor: influence and encirclement, not capture. Slow accumulation of position with no single decisive blow — a far better fit for gradual de-dollarisation than a checkmate.

A market / ecosystem

Interdependence, flows, feedback loops, no referee. This is the model the rest of this page uses — and the one the Dollar Engine above literally draws.

Iterated games

Repeated prisoner's dilemmas with reputation. Cooperation and defection play out over time; there is no final move, so today's betrayal is priced into tomorrow's deal.

Framing The deepest break: in chess the board is neutral and fixed. In geopolitics the board is the prize — the dollar is the board, and the whole contest is over who controls or replaces it. No chess move can represent that; it's exactly what the engine above is built to show. Keep the chess lens for who-is-allied-with-whom; reach for poker, Go or the market when you need bluffing, encirclement or interdependence.
