The script layer — living, classical, and dead hands, one per segmentV1.1

Type is a token too. Every segment carries its script — and where the script died with its court, the family revives it as a display face over the living one.

The leaf designed the script. The writing surface each region had is why its letters look the way they do — a material story the family keeps as a token rule: the segment’s type echoes its surface.

Palm leaf → the round scripts

A stylus dragged straight along a palm leaf splits it at the fiber — so Odia, Tamil, Malayalam, Telugu and Kannada bent every stroke into curves and hoops.

Birch bark → the sharp north

Bhojpatra from Himalayan birches takes ink from a reed pen — so Sharada and Devanagari kept straight strokes and the headline the pen could pull.

Sanchi bark → Assam’s hand

The aloe-bark folios of Kamarupa carried the Assamese letterforms — sized between leaf-curve and pen-line.

Copper plate → the chancery

Land grants chiselled in copper demanded compact, unambiguous forms — the discipline behind Modi, Nandinagari and grant-hand Grantha.

The flag layer — the dhvaja each lineage flew, kept as banner grammarV1.4

Every court raised a dhvaja — tiger, twin carp, bow, boar, sun, lion, Garuda. India read its banners like a language: shape meant intent, a raised flag meant a state had begun. The family keeps that grammar. Each standard below carries the UI role it becomes — and shows it, rendered live beside the flag: the swallowtail is our alert, the twin fish our paired badges, the hoisted kodi our time-boxed event. A banner is a status made visible; so is ours.

Sourced, or it's a gap. Standards are drawn from temple practice, chronicle and coinage; where a banner's exact form is reconstructed we mark it attested vs reconstructed. These are original geometric renderings, never the official emblem of any modern body.

The time layer — how temple design was designed, period by periodV0.1

A slow exploration we will keep growing: each period gets its sheet — what the builders changed, and which segment inherits it. Three of these already stand in 3D.

3rd c. BCE

Mauryan — the first stone

Barabar’s polished caves and the stupa railings: architecture learns stone from wood, joinery carved as if timber.

2nd c. BCE

Sanchi — the gateway

Torana gates rise over the stupa — the first monumental threshold; the gate outlives the fence.

4th–6th c.

Gupta — the first temples

The flat-roofed cell at Sanchi and Deogarh: garbhagriha + porch, the seed plan every later temple grows from.

6th–8th c.

The laboratory — Aihole to Mamallapuram

Chalukya workshops and the Pallava rathas prototype both families side by side: Nagara’s curve and Dravida’s steps split here.

8th c.

Karkota — Martand

Kashmir builds its sun temple in grey limestone: trefoil arcades, an 84-column court — the mountain register.

8th c.

Rashtrakuta — Kailasa

Ellora is cut downward out of the living ridge — design by removal, the plan complete before the first strike.

10th–11th c.

Chandela + Chola — the climax of height

Khajuraho clusters its spires into one mountain; Thanjavur stacks 13 dry-granite tiers to 216 ft (1010 CE).

13th c.

Ganga — Konark

The temple becomes a machine: 24 sundial wheels, seven horses, the whole mass aimed at first light (c. 1250).

15th c.

Mewar + Vijayanagara

Ranakpur’s 1444-pillar marble forest; Hampi’s granite bazaars — the temple becomes a city.

16th–17th c.

Nayaka — the gateway wins

Madurai’s gopurams tower over the shrine they guard — the threshold finally outgrows the sanctum, closing the arc Sanchi opened.
