How long each one lasted

Every bar is woven in its flag’s colours, from founding to the year it joined India or fell. Most kept their thrones through the Mughal centuries and under British paramountcy, until 1947 to 1950. Select a kingdom to read its flag.

Mewar

Sisodia, Guhila line, ruling from Udaipur. From c. 569.

Joined India, 1949

The Nishan: a triangular saffron flag on a red staff, with a gold sun bearing a human face and a silver katar dagger.

Sources differ. Sources differ on the field: the House of Mewar describes saffron; Flags of the World also records a dark purple and a “crimson” Nishan.

The tribal thread. The arms of Udaipur are supported by a Bhil and a Rajput warrior holding a shield with the sun.

Motto: “Jo dridh rakhe dharma ko, tihi rakhe kartar” — God protects the one who holds to dharma.

Marwar

Rathore, ruling from Jodhpur. From c. 1210.

Acceded to India, 7 April 1949

The Rajput panchranga: five equal horizontal stripes, saffron, white, red, yellow and green, with a white falcon for the tutelary goddess Durga.

Sources differ. Observers at Mehrangarh Fort recorded the stripes in a different order, orange, red, white, yellow, green, and sometimes without the falcon.

Capital moved from Mandore to Jodhpur, founded about 1450; a subsidiary alliance with the British from 6 January 1818.

Jaipur

Kachwaha, ruling from Amber, then Jaipur. From founding not yet sourced.

Joined India; date not yet sourced

From 1877, five stripes: red, green, white, blue and yellow, made for the assembly of princes at Queen Victoria’s proclamation as Empress.

Sources differ. The older flag of Man Singh, in 1630, was triangular, red, white, yellow, green and blue, said to mark victory over five Pathan chiefs.

Jaipur’s Raja Ram Singh I led the Mughal army against the Ahom at Saraighat in 1671.

Gwalior

Scindia (Shinde), Maratha, ruling from Gwalior. From 1731.

Merged into Madhya Bharat, 1948

Like the main Maratha houses, a saffron flag with a swallowtail; the arms carry a sun between two cobras.

Sources differ. Later state flags are recorded differently: saffron with a red diagonal band bearing sun and cobras, or a red over saffron bicolour.

The cobras recall a family legend of a cobra shielding the infant founder, Ranoji Scindia, from the sun.

#ef8a2a#c41e2a#f2ede2

Where this comes from

  • T4Flags of the World: Gwalior

Mysore

Wadiyar, ruling from Mysore. From founding not yet sourced.

Became Mysore State, 1950

Brick red over brown, with the arms in the centre: the two-headed Gandaberunda, today Karnataka’s emblem.

Sources differ. A plain red and brown version without arms circulates, but Flags of the World notes it was never official.

The Maharaja stayed head of Mysore State after 1950; the state was renamed Karnataka in 1974.

Where this comes from

Travancore

Travancore royal family, ruling from Padmanabhapuram, then Thiruvananthapuram. From 1729.

Formed Travancore-Cochin, 1 July 1949

Red, with a silver, right-coiled sacred conch in the centre.

The conch survives in the emblem of Kerala, adopted in 1960, held by two elephants.

Hyderabad

Asaf Jahi, ruling from Aurangabad, then Hyderabad. From 1724.

Annexed by India, 1948

The Nizam’s standard: yellow with a white disc in the centre.

Sources differ. Legend calls the disc a kulcha; Asaf Jah I said it was the moon, for his name Qamar. The sixth Nizam approved a design that confirmed the kulcha.

Placed under British protection in 1798, the first Indian prince to sign such an agreement.

Cooch Behar

Koch dynasty, ruling from Cooch Behar. From 1515.

Merged with India, 1949

Red, with a yellow sun in splendour.

The tribal thread. The name means land of the Koch: from the Koch tribes, and behar from vihara.

The Koch kingdom split in 1586 after Nara Narayan; Koch Bihar sided with the Mughals.

#c41e2a#efc23a

Where this comes from

  • T4Flags of the World: Cooch Behar

Tripura

Manikya, ruling from Udaipur, then Agartala. From c. 1400.

Merged with India, 15 October 1949

Red and yellow vertical halves, with a white disc in the centre carrying the ruler’s arms. The arms are left blank here.

Sources differ. Three accounts disagree: red and yellow split 1:3; two equal red and yellow halves with a white disc; or purple and gold split 3:1 with the emblem in a white disc.

The tribal thread. The Twipra kingdom was one of the largest historical kingdoms of the Tripuri people.

Maha Manikya took the throne in 1400 as the first king with the title Manikya.

#c41e2a#efc23a#f2ede2

Where this comes from

  • T4Flags of the World: Tripura, after Ziggioto (1998) and Haynes

Manipur

Ningthouja, ruling from Kangla, Imphal. From founding not yet sourced.

Taken into India, 1949

White and rectangular, with the sacred emblem of Pakhangba, the dragon god, in the centre. Drawn here only as a schematic coil.

Sources differ. The seven-coloured Salai Taret flag stands for the seven Meitei clans; it is disputed as a flag for all of Manipur, because many communities there are not of those clans.

The tribal thread. Manipur’s listed Scheduled Tribes are Naga and Kuki groups; the Meitei are not on the list.

At midnight on 14 August 1947 the flag replaced the Union Jack at Kangla; the original is in the Manipur State Museum.

Ahom

Ahom, founded by Sukaphaa, ruling from Garhgaon and others. From 1228.

Annexed by the British, 1826

No flag description is in the atlas yet. The Ngi-ngao-kham, a winged lion-dragon of the Tai-Ahom, stands at Lachit Borphukan’s memorial.

In March 1671 Lachit Borphukan’s forces beat the Mughal army on the Brahmaputra at Saraighat, and Mughal advance into Assam stopped.

Where this comes from

  • T2Indian Culture portal: Battle of Saraighat
  • T4Live History India: Lachit Borphukan

Goa

Kadamba, then Delhi, Vijayanagara, Bahmani and Bijapur rule, ruling from Old Goa. From Kadamba from the 2nd century.

Taken by Portugal, 25 November 1510

No flag of Goa’s pre-1510 rulers is in the atlas yet.

The tribal thread. Goa’s listed tribes today include the Gawda, Kunbi and Velip.

Portuguese rule lasted about 450 years, until 1961.

  • T2Goa Dept of Information and Publicity: History of Goa

Rathore (Bika line), ruling from Bikaner. From 1488.

Joined Greater Rajasthan, 1949

Red over saffron, with the state arms in the centre: a white falcon on a shield, on a yellow pedestal under a yellow crown. The falcon is drawn here as a schematic.

Jaisalmer

Bhati, ruling from Jaisalmer. From 1156.

Joined Greater Rajasthan, 1949

Red over purple, with a white parasol (chhatri) in the centre, trimmed in gold.

Sources differ. Some describe the upper band as saffron red, and proportions as 1:2 or 2:3. A flag seen at the fort in 2004 was yellow over red, with the parasol in inverted colours.

Bundi

Hada Chauhan, ruling from Bundi. From founding not yet sourced.

Joined the Rajasthan Union, 1948

The 1935 flag: three equal bands of red, white and yellow, with a red stripe at the hoist. In the white band, red figures: a reversed crescent, a moon, crossed Rajput daggers and a motto label.

Patiala

Phulkian (Sidhu), ruling from Patiala. From founding not yet sourced.

Formed PEPSU with neighbouring states, 1948

Seven equal stripes: red, green, yellow, red, yellow, green, red. In the fly, a vertical sabre with a yellow hilt and white blade, over a black shield with five small white discs.

Bhopal

Orakzai Pashtun, later ruled by the Begums, ruling from Bhopal. From founding not yet sourced.

Merged with India, 1949

The Begum’s standard: black, white and green bands with a yellow fringe, a silver crown in the top band, and gold lettering and a tughra in the middle band. The lettering is drawn here as a single stroke.

Sources differ. This is the ruler’s standard. The state flag appears only as an image in our source, so its description is still missing.

Four women ruled Bhopal in succession as Begums for most of the 19th and early 20th centuries.

Merged into Madhya Bharat, 1948

Red over white, with the state arms near the hoist reaching into the lower band. The arms are drawn here only as a gold roundel.

Baroda

Gaekwad, Maratha, ruling from Vadodara. From founding not yet sourced.

Merged into Bombay State, 1949

A saffron swallow-tailed flag, the shape shared by the Maratha states. From 1936 the standard added a crown above a scimitar (tulwar) in the centre.

Sources differ. Earlier standards (to 1874, and 1874–1936) differ in detail; the shade is said to match Gwalior’s.

On ships, Baroda’s badge was a mounted trooper, a scimitar and the word BARODA in white on red ochre.

Maratha

Bhonsle, then the Peshwas, ruling from Raigad, Satara, then Pune. From 1674.

Fell to the British, 1818

The Bhagwa Jhenda: a plain saffron swallow-tailed flag. Baroda, Gwalior, Sangli and other Maratha states flew versions of it.

The tribal thread. Koli, Bhil and Ramoshi fighters held many of the hill forts the Maratha state was built on.

Kutch

Jadeja, ruling from Bhuj. From founding not yet sourced.

Joined India; centrally governed from 1948

The merchant flag: red, with a white crescent and a radiant sun. The state banner was red with a white crest of Bhuj fort, crescent, crown and elephant, over a mount bearing the fish (mahi maratib).

Sources differ. A second banner is recorded: red with a black-and-white castle, crown, a BHUJ scroll and an elephant rider.

Sikkim

Namgyal (Chogyal), ruling from Yuksom, Rabdentse, Tumlong, then Gangtok. From 1641 or 1642.

Became India’s 22nd state, 1975

From 1967: white, with a red border all round and a yellow eight-spoked khorlo wheel, edged in red, in the centre.

Sources differ. Earlier flags (1877 to 1967) had a red, white and blue triple border and religious emblems around a red wheel; they were simplified because they were hard to copy.

The tribal thread. The Lepcha, Bhutia and Limbu are among Sikkim’s listed tribes.

Jammu and Kashmir

Dogra, ruling from Jammu and Srinagar. From 1846.

Acceded to India, October 1947

From 1936: red, with a white plough. The plough is drawn here as a schematic.

Sources differ. From 1846 the flag was plain saffron-red with a rounded tail. The state flag used from 1952 to 2019 added three white stripes at the hoist for Jammu, Kashmir and Ladakh.

Thrones and the tribes beside them

Several of these kingdoms carry tribal peoples in their names, their arms or their founding. The link runs both ways: Mangarh, in 1913, was a massacre of Bhil followers on the border of two princely states.

Mewar and the Bhil

A Bhil warrior supports the shield of Udaipur’s arms, beside a Rajput.

Cooch Behar and the Koch

The state is named for the Koch tribes; Koch Rajbongshi people trace back to the kingdom.

Tripura and the Tripuri

The Manikya kings ruled the Twipra kingdom of the Tripuri people until 1949.

Banswara, Sunth and Mangarh

Troops of the princely states and the British fired on Govind Guru’s Bhil assembly on 17 November 1913.

Goa and its first peoples

Gawda, Kunbi and Velip are on Goa’s list of Scheduled Tribes today.
