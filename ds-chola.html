The story — how we designed it

inspired-by · origins · decisions

What it's inspired from

The Chola register is drawn from the Tamil imperial age (9th–13th c. CE) — the era of the great bronze Nataraja and the towering Brihadisvara temple at Thanjavur. Its touchstones are cast metal and monumental granite: the lost-wax bronzes aged to green patina, the golden vimana, Tamil inscription, and the kumkum red of active worship.

What we know of its origins

The Cholas built a maritime empire reaching Southeast Asia, and a bronze-casting tradition held among the finest metal sculpture ever made. The Great Living Chola Temples are a UNESCO World Heritage site — 'living' because worship never stopped, so the palette is not archaeology but current practice: ash-white, granite, bronze, and vermilion, observed as much as excavated.

The design decisions that followed

From that heritage we made concrete choices, each traceable to a source:

  • Sacred ash as paper. The ground is vibhuti white (--background) — the temple's own surface.
  • Kumkum as action. The one action colour is temple vermilion, because in the culture it already means 'here, now, sacred'.
  • Bronze as ink; patina as success. Text is bronze-brown; the success hue is the verdigris of aged metal.
  • Monumental corner. Radius stays a squared 3px — granite blocks, not soft plastic.
  • Kaveri teal as the quiet second voice. A river-water blue-green carries secondary data.

Every colour below carries its own origin story — click any swatch marked “story” to see where the pigment came from and the reference behind it. A colour here is a place with a history, not a decoration.

Standards & legacy

how the empire held the line

The Cholas ran an empire on records — and their canon is literally carved into the walls. A legacy is not an accident; it is a standard, maintained, and audited. Here is how the Tamil imperium held the line.

The temple as a module

The great temples were built to proportion — the vimana rising in measured, self-similar tiers. Brihadisvara at Thanjavur is a single coherent module scaled to monumental size — order you can measure.

→ our layout scale & elevation ladder: one modular system, scaled up cleanly.

Inscribed & audited

Chola temple walls carry thousands of inscriptions — endowments, land grants, wages, audits — a public ledger in stone that held officials to a recorded standard.

→ our sourced-or-gap ledger: every figure traceable, nothing unattributed.

A canon in bronze

The lost-wax bronzes repeated an iconographic canon so consistently that a Chola Nataraja is unmistakable — a house style enforced across generations of workshops.

→ our atoms & finish tokens: one recognisable house form, cast the same each time.

Sourced: the Great Living Chola Temples (UNESCO) and the vast corpus of Chola temple inscriptions studied since the ASI's early epigraphy; Chola bronze iconography. Read from surviving stone and metal — Chola art ↗ · the temples

The method

how to read this page

Why atomic, and how we decide the tier

Atomic Design (Brad Frost) borrows a chemistry metaphor: interfaces are built from atoms that combine into molecules, which assemble into organisms, laid into templates. We add a tier 0 · Tokens underneath — the raw values (colour, type, space, elevation) that everything else is made of. The point isn't taxonomy for its own sake; it's reuse: fix a token once and the whole system reskins — which is exactly how one scaffold serves Mauryan, Gupta, and a design system per state.

The decision rule we use on every specimen:

Atom — cannot be broken down and still do its job. A <button>, an input, a badge, a rule, the spinner. It has no meaning of its own beyond its form. Molecule — a small, reusable group of atoms that together do one job: a label + input + error is a field; a set of atoms bonded to a single purpose. If removing one atom breaks the purpose, it's a molecule, not a coincidental grouping. Organism — a distinct, self-contained section: a table, a dialog, a nav bar. It can stand alone on a page. Template — the page-level skeleton where organisms are placed; content-agnostic layout.

Each specimen below is tagged with its tier and — where the call is interesting — a line on why.

Tokens

the raw material

Why tokens sit below the atoms

An atom is a thing (a button); a token is a value (the gold it's painted with). Tokens are the only place a hex, a font, or a pixel is allowed to be literal. Everything upward references them, so a skin is nothing more than a different set of token values on the same atoms. Never hard-code — reference the variable. Chola values come from real heritage: bronze and its verdigris patina, Thanjavur's golden granite, and the temple's kumkum red. The ground is sacred-ash (vibhuti) white; the action colour is kumkum vermilion; corners stay a monumental 3px — granite blocks, not soft plastic.

Colour

token

Granite & bronze. Ash-white paper, bronze-brown ink, kumkum vermilion for action, patina green for success.

Type scale

token

One modular scale. Display serif for headlines, sans for body, mono for labels & data. Use the step, never an ad-hoc pixel. The Brihadisvara vimana rises in thirteen measured tiers to 216 feet, each storey a fixed step of the last. This type ramp climbs the same way — proportion by the tier, never an ad-hoc pixel.

Spacing · radius · elevation

token

A 4px rhythm; a tight radius (the Mauryan register drops corners to an incised 2px); one shadow ladder. Consistency here is what makes layouts feel calm. The corner stays a monumental 3px — Chola form is dry-stacked granite, block on block without mortar. Soft radii would betray the stone; the edge holds because the stone does.

Atoms

indivisible primitives

Why these are atoms

Each thing here does one job and can't be split without losing it. A button is still a button with nothing inside but a word; a badge is a coloured word; a rule is a line. They carry no content logic — you drop them anywhere. If you find yourself giving an "atom" a heading and a body and a control, it stopped being an atom two additions ago.

Buttons

atom

The primary action wears the house gold; dark = stone-ink; ghost = incised outline. The action colour is kumkum vermilion — the sacred red of active worship, dabbed on the deity and the devotee. In the culture it already means here, now, do this; so it means it here too.

atom

Custom visuals over a real native input, so keyboard and screen-reader semantics stay free. A mark made and kept — struck into the temple wall like the mason's guild-sign. The switch is the sanctum lamp lit or dark; the checkbox, the chisel-tick that certifies a course complete.

Molecules

atoms bonded to one job

Why these are molecules, not atoms

Each is several atoms bonded so tightly that the group is the reusable unit. A field is a label + input + help/error — pull the label off and the input loses its accessible name; the bond is the point. A segmented control is buttons that only mean something as a mutually-exclusive set. The test: if the atoms are only meaningful together, and you'd reuse them together, it's a molecule. A molecule still isn't a whole page section — that's the next tier up.

Field (label + control + help/error)

molecule

The smallest genuinely useful form unit — and the clearest atom-vs-molecule example on the page. Label, control and helper bound as one — like a Chola endowment carved in stone: the grant, the land it governs, and the terms beneath. No clause stands alone; the record is the whole.

Segmented control · tabs · pagination

Organisms

standalone sections

Why these are organisms

An organism is a distinct, self-sufficient part of an interface — you could point at it on a page and name it: "the data table," "the dialog," "the card grid," "the toolbar." It composes molecules and atoms, owns a chunk of layout, and often owns behaviour. The line from molecule to organism: a molecule is a control; an organism is a place.

Templates & patterns

where organisms live

Why templates are the top tier

A template is the page-level skeleton: it arranges organisms into a layout without caring what content fills them. It's the difference between "a card grid" (organism) and "the front-door layout: hero, then a start-here row, then topic sections" (template). Our real templates already ship as pages — home.html, the article layout, the atlas shell. This tier is the bridge from the system to the product.
